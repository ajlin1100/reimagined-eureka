This guide covers how to write and format ServiceNow knowledge articles. Section 1 covers the basics of writing knowledge content; Section 2 documents the department formatting standard; Section 3 is a copy-paste library of the HTML elements used in our published articles; Section 4 covers the authoring tools and local editing workflow; Section 5 walks through authoring an article in ServiceNow step by step. This document practices what it preaches: it is built entirely from the elements it describes.

1. Knowledge Article Basics

1.1 When to write a knowledge article

A knowledge article answers one question or walks the reader through one task. Good candidates are repeat help-desk questions, stable procedures (ordering, account setup, classroom technology), and policy or reference material that readers return to. If the content changes week to week, applies to exactly one person, or is really several unrelated tasks, it does not belong in a single KB article.

1.2 Before you write

  • Search first. Look for existing articles on the same topic. Update or expand an existing article rather than creating a near-duplicate; duplicates split search results and go stale independently.
  • Identify the audience. Smith School faculty, staff, and students read differently than internal staff. Choose vocabulary, depth, and prerequisites for the least technical intended reader.
  • Verify the procedure end to end. Walk every step yourself in a current environment. Capture screenshots as you go.
  • Collect links and references. Vendor documentation, related articles, forms, and contact points belong in the article, not in the reader's inbox.

1.3 Anatomy of an article

Field Guidance
Title Use the words a reader would search for. Name the system and the task, e.g., Shell Shop Order in Workday. Avoid internal jargon and ticket-speak.
Short description One or two sentences: what the article does and who it is for. This text appears in search results, so make it stand alone.
Body Follow the department skeleton: banner, title, optional table of contents, sections, optional footer. For procedures: overview → prerequisites → numbered steps → verification → related links.
Keywords / meta Add synonyms and common misspellings readers might search (e.g., “Shell Shop,” “ShellShop,” “storefront”). Keywords decide whether the article is found.
Category & owner Assign the correct knowledge category and a named owner. The owner is responsible for accuracy, review, and retirement.

1.4 Writing style rules

  • Write in second person (“you”), active voice, present tense: “Click Checkout”, not “The Checkout button should be clicked.”
  • Number procedural steps and keep one action per step. If a step needs two actions, split it.
  • Bold the names of buttons, fields, and menu items exactly as they appear on screen, e.g., “Review Required”.
  • Put warnings and prerequisites before the step they affect, never after.
  • Keep paragraphs short (2–4 sentences). Use lists for anything enumerable.
  • Define acronyms on first use: “Purchase Order (PUR)”.
  • Screenshots must show the current interface, be cropped to the relevant area, and carry alt text and a caption.

1.5 Article lifecycle

Every article has an owner and a review date. Owners re-verify procedures when the underlying system changes (Workday releases, portal updates), act on reader feedback, and retire or merge articles that are superseded. An outdated article is worse than none — it erodes trust in the whole knowledge base.

2. Department Formatting Guidelines

These guidelines distill the Smith brand standards (see sp2026-rebuild/standards/) into the constraints of the ServiceNow knowledge editor, as demonstrated in the department's reference articles (see Section 7).

2.1 ServiceNow constraints

Note: Inline Styles Only

The ServiceNow knowledge editor strips <style> blocks, external stylesheets, and JavaScript. All formatting must be inline via the style attribute. Class names such as smith-it-banner are kept only as semantic markers for authors; they carry no styling on their own.

Images must be uploaded as article attachments and referenced by attachment file name (e.g., umd-formal-seal.png) or by /sys_attachment.do?sys_id=... URL. Add rel="nofollow" to hyperlinks.

2.2 Brand palette

Use these tokens consistently. Red is for emphasis, structure, and critical notices — never for long passages of text.

2.4 Article skeleton

Every article is assembled in this order. Sections 3.1–3.3 give the full code for each block.

  1. Header banner (smith-it-banner) — office identity and contact block.
  2. Body wrapper (smith-body-wrapper) — all remaining content goes inside; sets the Verdana stack, 12pt body text, and 800px centered column.
  3. Title (h1) — one per article.
  4. TOC nav — optional; include for articles with three or more sections.
  5. Sections (h2/h3) — prose, lists, annotations, images, and tables. Separate major sections with a horizontal rule where it aids scanning.
  6. Footer block — optional quick-reference or contact panel.

2.5 Accessibility requirements

  • Every image has descriptive alt text that names what the screenshot shows.
  • Link text describes the destination (“Procurement Codes Sheet”), never “click here”.
  • Never communicate meaning with color alone. Every callout pairs its color with an explicit text label (“Note”, “PROHIBITED”).
  • Keep heading order strict: one h1, then h2, then h3 — no skipped levels.
  • Use real tables with header cells for tabular data. The borderless media table is the one sanctioned layout-table pattern, for pairing a screenshot with its walkthrough.

4. Authoring Tools

The ServiceNow knowledge editor is adequate for plain text, but it is a difficult environment for HTML formatting work: the editing surface is small, there is no find-and-replace, no syntax highlighting, and the rich-text layer can rewrite or strip markup on save. The department's standard practice is to author and format article HTML in a local code editor — typically Visual Studio Code — and paste the finished markup into ServiceNow's HTML source view.

4.1 Recommended editors

Tool Why use it
Visual Studio Code (recommended) Full HTML syntax highlighting and tag matching, regex find-and-replace, multi-cursor editing, Emmet abbreviation expansion, bracket-pair colorization, and a large extension ecosystem. Free and available on managed workstations.
Notepad++ Lightweight Windows alternative with HTML highlighting and regex search. Suitable for quick edits when VS Code is unavailable.
Any plain-text editor The workflow only requires an editor that saves raw text. What matters is that markup is edited as text, not through a rich-text canvas.
DO NOT

Author or copy article content through Microsoft Word, Outlook, or other rich-text applications. They inject proprietary markup (<o:p>, mso-* styles, nested spans) that bloats the article, breaks the department style, and is difficult to remove.

4.2 The round-trip workflow

  1. Extract. In ServiceNow, open the article and switch the body field to HTML source view (the </> control). Copy the full markup.
  2. Save locally. Paste into a new .html file in your editor. Keep one file per article, named for the article (e.g., kb0011076-new.html).
  3. Edit. Apply the elements from Section 3. Use find-and-replace for repetitive style updates (e.g., retargeting every link color).
  4. Preview. Open the file in a browser to check rendering. For an accurate preview, wrap the article markup in a minimal shell with the banner and body wrapper from Section 2.4 — this document is itself an example of that pattern.
  5. Publish. Paste the finished markup back into the ServiceNow HTML source view, save, and verify the result in the portal preview — not just the editor, which renders differently.
Note: ServiceNow May Normalize Markup on Save

The editor can reorder attributes, collapse whitespace, or drop elements it does not allow. Always diff the saved article against your local file after publishing, and treat the local file as the source of truth for future edits — never re-copy markup out of ServiceNow as your starting point.

4.3 Useful VS Code capabilities for KB work

  • Find and replace with regex (Ctrl+H, enable .*): update a style token across every element at once, e.g., replace all #0056b3 link colors.
  • Multi-cursor editing (Alt+Click / Ctrl+Alt+Down): apply the same attribute change to several list items or table cells simultaneously.
  • Emmet: expand abbreviations such as ul>li*3 into list skeletons, then add inline styles.
  • Tag matching and folding: click a <div> to highlight its closer; fold nested blocks to navigate long articles. Mismatched tags are flagged inline.
  • Preview extensions (e.g., a live HTML preview or Live Server): watch the rendered article update as you edit, without switching to a browser manually.
  • Source control: keep article files in Git so every formatting change is tracked, diffable, and reversible. The reference articles under articles/procurement/ in this workspace follow that practice.

4.4 Reformat an existing article with GitHub Copilot

GitHub Copilot Chat in VS Code can help apply the existing formatting standard to an article's HTML. Treat it as an editing assistant: provide the relevant guidelines as context, limit the requested changes, and review the result before publishing.

  1. Set up Copilot. In VS Code, install the GitHub Copilot and GitHub Copilot Chat extensions if they are not already available. (No company account currently exists for this, so use a Free Account if you want to utilize AI to speed up the formatting process.)
  2. Make the style guides available. Access the shared cloud folder containing the Smith Style Guide and any related page-type guidance, and download them into one of your VS Code workspace subfolders. Use the VS Code Workspace template folder as the starting point for your workspace.
  3. Open a working copy. Save the existing article HTML as a separate local working file so the original remains unchanged. Open that file in VS Code.
  4. Add focused chat context. In Copilot Chat, use Add Context to attach the article, the relevant files from the shared style-guide folder, and this guide's Sections 2 and 3. You can also use #file references when available. Follow company data-handling rules and use only an approved account for article content.
  5. Request a constrained reformat. Ask Copilot to preserve the article's meaning and content while updating its presentation to match the supplied guidelines. For example:
Reformat this existing ServiceNow knowledge article using the attached KB formatting guidance and Smith style guides. Preserve all wording, facts, steps, links, and images; change presentation and HTML formatting only. Keep all styles inline and do not add scripts, style blocks, external stylesheets, or unsupported elements. Do not invent or remove information. Flag anything that needs an editorial decision instead of changing it. Show me the proposed changes so I can review them.
  1. Review and verify. Inspect every proposed change in the diff, especially links, images, wording, and inline styles. Reject changes that alter article content or conflict with ServiceNow constraints. Preview the HTML in a browser, then follow the round-trip workflow above and verify the published portal preview.

5. Authoring an Article in ServiceNow, Step by Step

This section walks a new article through ServiceNow from record creation to published result. It assumes the planning in Section 1 is complete: the formatting standard is Section 2, the markup blocks are Section 3, and the local editing workflow is Section 4.

5.1 Create the article record

  1. Open a new article. In ServiceNow, navigate to Knowledge > Articles > Create New (or open the knowledge base and select New Article).
  2. Choose the knowledge base. The knowledge base determines the available categories, the publication workflow, and the article's audience — confirm it before writing anything else.
  3. Save the draft immediately. ServiceNow assigns the KB number on first save. Record it and name your local HTML file after it, so the published article and its source file are always paired.

5.2 Complete the article fields

Section 1.3 covers what to write in each field. On the ServiceNow form, also note:

Field ServiceNow notes
Title & short description Both appear in portal search results; write them for a reader scanning a results list.
Category The category list is filtered by the selected knowledge base. Choose the most specific category that fits — readers browse by category.
Owner The owner receives feedback tasks and review reminders. Name an individual so accountability is clear.
Keywords / meta Add synonyms and common misspellings. Keywords affect search only; they are not shown on the published article.
Review date Set the date by which the owner re-verifies the procedure.

5.3 Build the body

  1. Draft locally. Compose and format the body in your code editor following Sections 2 and 3, per the round-trip workflow. Never compose in the rich-text editor or paste from Word or Outlook.
  2. Open the HTML source view. In the article body field, click the </> control to switch from rich text to source.
  3. Paste the finished markup. Replace the entire contents of the source view with the markup from your local file.
  4. Attach images. Save the record, then upload each screenshot as an article attachment. Reference each image by attachment file name or /sys_attachment.do?sys_id=... URL, with alt text, dimensions, and a caption.
  5. Save from the source view. Save the record while still in HTML source view.
Note: Stay in HTML Source View Until You Save

Switching back to the rich-text view before saving lets the editor rewrite your markup — Section 4.2 describes the normalization ServiceNow applies. If you want to check the rich-text rendering, save first, then reopen the article.

5.4 Preview, review, and publish

  1. Preview in the portal. Open the article as a reader will see it. The editor renders differently from the portal, so the editor alone is not a valid check.
  2. Run the checklist. Work through the pre-publication checklist.
  3. Get a second reader. A second person proofreads the article before publication.
  4. Publish. Submit the article through your knowledge base's publication workflow and confirm the workflow state shows Published.
  5. Verify after publication. Search the portal by title and by keywords; confirm the article surfaces, images render, and links work. Diff the saved markup against your local source of truth.

6. Pre-Publication Checklist

Content

  • The article answers one question or completes one task; the title uses words readers actually search for.
  • The short description states what the article does and who it is for.
  • Every procedure was walked end to end in a current environment; steps are numbered with one action each.
  • Warnings and prerequisites appear before the steps they affect; acronyms are defined on first use.

Formatting

  • Banner, body wrapper, and heading hierarchy (h1 → h2 → h3) follow Section 2; no skipped heading levels.
  • All styling is inline; there are no <style> blocks, scripts, or external stylesheets.
  • Links are underlined blue with descriptive text and rel="nofollow".
  • Callouts are used sparingly and each carries an explicit text label.
  • Articles with three or more sections include a TOC nav, and every TOC link resolves to a heading id.

ServiceNow

  • Images are attached to the article, render in the portal preview (not just the editor), and have alt text and captions.
  • Keywords/meta, category, and owner are set; a review date is assigned.
  • A local copy of the article HTML is saved as the source of truth before publishing.
  • A second person has proofread the article before publication.
